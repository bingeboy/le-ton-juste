# Bob Weir — 1970s Hi-Fi Tone

## How Bob Differed from Jerry

Bob Weir's approach to the hi-fi rig was fundamentally different from Jerry's despite sharing the same amplification backbone (Alembic + McIntosh + JBL).

| Aspect | Jerry Garcia | Bob Weir |
|---|---|---|
| Role | Lead guitar, melodic single-note lines | Rhythm guitar, complex jazz voicings |
| Guitar | Custom Alembic/Irwin solid body, single coils → humbuckers | Gibson ES-335 (1965 cherry), ES-345, Gibson SG — all humbuckers |
| Preamp | Alembic F-2B (Fender-type tone stack) | Alembic Parametric Equalizer (surgical EQ) |
| Effects | Relatively dry — wah, Mu-Tron, octave divider | Much wetter — Eventide digital delay, Eventide phaser, Orban/Parasound studio reverb |
| Tone | Bright, articulate, scooped mids | Fuller, midrange-heavy, shimmery |
| Picking | Hybrid picking (pick + fingers), banjo rolls | Flatpick strumming, percussive right hand |

## Weir's Signal Chain (Wall of Sound Era)

```
Gibson ES-335 (humbuckers)
  → Effects (Eventide delay, Eventide phaser, modulation)
  → Alembic Parametric EQ
  → Orban/Parasound Studio Reverb
  → McIntosh MC2300
  → JBL Column
```

## Key Distinctions

### Humbucker Foundation

Bob's Gibson semi-hollows with PAF-style humbuckers provided a fundamentally darker, thicker starting point than Jerry's bright single-coil or custom humbucker instruments. The semi-hollow body added resonance and acoustic bloom.

### Surgical EQ

While Jerry used a Fender-style passive tone stack (Bass, Mid, Treble, Bright switch), Bob used an Alembic Parametric EQ — fully sweepable frequency, bandwidth, and boost/cut per band. This gave him precise control over his tone for chord work.

### Much More Effects

Bob's tone was characterized by:
- **Eventide Digital Delay:** Clean, rhythmic repeats, almost shimmer-like
- **Eventide Phaser:** Sweeping modulation, lush chordal movement
- **Studio-grade reverb:** Orban/Parasound units were broadcast/studio processors, not guitar gear

### Jazz Voicings

Bob consciously took on a "harmonic bridge" role between Phil Lesh's bass and Jerry's lead. He played chord voicings that would normally require a keyboard — McCoy Tyner-influenced quartal harmony, wide-interval voicings. This required:
- Clean, clear amplification so complex chords don't get muddy
- Extended frequency response (JBLs) so high and low chord tones both speak
- Subtle reverb to glue the chords without washing out the voicings

## Takeaways for Our Rig

- The Thick Air + Dirt combination should be capable of both Jerry-style lead boost AND Bob-style chord enhancement
- The QuadraVerb can handle the Eventide-style modulation and delay that Bob used
- EQ approach: For Bob tones, less mid-scoop, more parametric/balanced EQ
- Reverb needs to be subtle enough to not muddy complex jazz voicings
- The clean McIntosh + JBL setup serves both approaches equally well — it's the upstream voicing that changes

## Bob's Influence on the Clean Tone Philosophy

While Jerry is often credited with the Alembic/McIntosh approach, Bob was equally committed to it. His reasoning was practical: with complex chord voicings, you need a rig that doesn't compress, distort, or color the harmonic relationships between notes. The hi-fi approach isn't just about "sounding good" — it's about making complex musical information intelligible.
